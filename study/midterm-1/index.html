1. What is strategy

Definition (use this wording)

Strategy is a theory and a set of choices that helps a company generate and sustain a competitive advantage.

The four strategic choices (she flagged this slide "important")

#ChoiceQuestion it answers
1Where to competeWhich markets (industries or geographic markets) for a high return on invested capital? Who do we serve?
2Unique valueWhat unique value do we offer customers in those markets? (Why we win with customers)
3Resources and capabilitiesWhat assets, brands, processes will let us deliver that value better than rivals?
4Sustain the valueWhat are the barriers to imitation? How do we stop competitors from copying us?

The whole course is organized around these four. External analysis answers #1. Generic strategies and innovation answer #2. Internal analysis answers #3 and #4. Corporate strategy and global strategy return to #1.

Her take (9/2)

Resources are the ingredients; capabilities are the recipe (how you combine them). Two students had the same candy and got different results because of negotiation, social capital and information. She added: strategy is about gaining and sustaining, and "it's not enough to create. You also want to be able to capture."

Three principles (taught with scenarios on 9/2)

  • Context-dependent, with trade-offs. Saying yes to something means saying no to something else. Not advertising can be fine (IKEA barely does) or bad, depending on context.
  • Choices must fit together (alignment). Luxury brands (Birkin bag, Coach) discount badly if their value comes from scarcity. Discounting is fine when it fits the rest of the strategy.
  • Gain and sustain, and think about competitors. Delta and American Express offered triple miles. Rivals copied it, it became a race to the bottom, and the whole industry got less profitable.

Mission, objectives, strategy, organization

Mission = fundamental purpose and values. Objectives = specific targets. Strategy = the choices that achieve them (the focus of this course). Organization = structure, systems, staff that implement it. Amazon: mission "number one customer-centered company"; strategy "huge selection, one-stop shop"; organization "local fulfillment centers".

Study of business-unit return on assets (Roquebert et al., 1996). This slide is repeated as a divider through the semester and she called it a "most important" slide.

2. Problem solving

Study guide items: 4S framework, TOSCA, issue-driven vs hypothesis-driven problem solving, design thinking.

Why bother: four pitfalls (one per step)

PitfallExampleLesson
Poor problem definition (State)Record labels asked "how do we stop illegal sharing to protect CD sales?" Apple asked "how can we make money in a world where distribution is changing?" and built iTunes.Reframing the question changes the solution space. "A problem well-stated is half-solved."
Narrow framing / wrong mental model (Structure)JCPenney's new CEO from Apple Retail cut coupons and discounts. Customers hated it and he was fired.He transferred a mental model from a different company.
Solution confirmation (Solve)Grameen–Danone yogurt for malnourished children in Bangladesh failed (lactose intolerance, refrigeration, dairy was a monthly treat).Don't just run with the solution you already believe. Test and challenge it.
Miscommunication (Sell)Fat vs sugar: the intuitive "fat makes you fat" beat the British scientist's complex sugar story.Communicating effectively matters as much as being right.

Two general traps: analysis paralysis and jumping to solutions. She noted experts fall into them more, because they assume they know what worked in the past.

4S method

  1. State the problem
  2. Structure the problem
  3. Solve the problem
  4. Sell the solution

State it with TOSCA

Memory device: the opera Tosca. Mario is jailed; Tosca wants him out but won't do the things Scarpia (the police chief who decides Mario's fate) demands.

LetterStepWhat to writeTosca
TTroubleThe symptoms: facts, not questions. A gap between reality and aspiration, as specific as possible.Mario is in jail
OOwnerWhose problem is it? One owner (can be a collective). The owner choice shapes everything else. Be precise: "top management," not "the company."Tosca
SSuccess criteriaMetrics, amount, timing, who judges.Mario out, alive, tomorrow
CConstraintsPrior commitments, resource limits, time limits.Her morals
AActorsWho else has a say? The ones that matter can block your solution.Scarpia

Combine them into one core question. Example: "In a context where consumers increasingly download pirated music, what can our company do to restore X% revenue growth with a minimum return on sales of Y% in three years?" Specific wording narrows the solution space (rules out suing teenagers).

Her take (9/8, 9/16)

Her parking example: vague "improve parking at BYU" became "How can BYU Parking Services raise student satisfaction with campus parking from 60% to 75% over three years without building more structures or raising its budget?" Takeaway: problems should be stated specifically and ideally hit all five topics. Put the result as a question, because a question tells your brain "problem solving." (The transcript sometimes writes TOSCA as "TSCA"; same framework.)

Structure it: three approaches (this is what the exam asks you to choose between)

ApproachHow it worksUse when
Issue tree (issue-driven)Start with the core question and split it into sub-issues (each phrased as a question). Branches must be MECE: mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (no gaps).The default, "whenever you can." You don't know the answer and want to explore comprehensively. Also lets you divide work among teammates.
Hypothesis pyramid (hypothesis-driven)Start with a candidate answer (leading hypothesis), build sub-hypotheses to confirm or disconfirm it.You have strong reasons or expertise to start with a solution; a simple yes/no question (e.g. "should we expand to China?"); time pressure. Risk: falling into solution confirmation.
Design thinkingObserve and talk to real users before building any tree or hypothesis. (Standard steps: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test.)You don't understand the problem well enough yet, especially human behavior (e.g. "why do people stop using fitness wearables?").
Her take: the practice question (10/28)

Library visits are declining: which approach? Any of the three earns full credit if you justify it. Hypothesis-driven: "we already have a good idea what the problem is, we test one hypothesis." Issue-driven: "we don't know, so break it into branches." Design thinking: "we have no clue why this behavior happens, so observe people first." Then give a specific first step. This is on the study guide.

Exam trap

The study guide says "issue-driven"; in lectures she calls it the issue tree. Same thing. MECE example from class: Starbucks France, a plain "increase revenue vs reduce cost" tree is less insightful and not truly mutually exclusive (more volume raises some costs).

Solve and Sell (lighter weight)

  • Solve: make an analysis plan (sub-issue → analysis → source → who). Pick the right data (GE's CEO looked bad on stock price, but he started just before 9/11). Make assumptions explicit. Ask "so what?" (AI use correlates with better financials because already-successful firms invest in AI: correlation is not causation). Beware survivor bias ("who are we not looking at?").
  • Sell: the pyramid principle: core message first, then support. Two storylines: grouping (core message + parallel reasons; easy to follow, good for receptive audiences) and argument (situation → complication → resolution; better for skeptical audiences and hard-to-sell conclusions).

3. External analysis

Study guide: Five Forces (industry attractiveness; rivalry, buyer power, supplier power, threat of entry, threat of substitutes, complements); industry concentration vs fragmentation; HHI / four-firm concentration ratio; switching costs; barriers to entry; PESTEL.

Key definition

Industry attractiveness = the degree to which the average firm in an industry can earn profits. Weak forces = attractive; strong forces = unattractive.

Exam trap #1 (she called this one out)

Five Forces explains differences between industries (why soft drinks beat airlines). It cannot explain why Coke beats Pepsi: a new entrant or substitute threatens both equally, so Five Forces gives the same answer for each. Within-industry differences come from internal analysis (resources, capabilities, strategy). She said "even a lot of MBA students get confused on" this. You missed the matching question on Quiz 1.

Industry concentration

ConcentratedFragmented

HHINear 10,000 (monopoly)Near 0
Typical rivalry and profitTends to be more profitableTends to be less profitable
  • C4 = sales of the four largest firms ÷ total industry sales. HHI = 10,000 × the sum of each firm's squared market share (uses all firms, weights big firms more). The two measures are highly correlated.
  • Slide practice: nine firms (six at $10, two at $5, one at $20). Top four = 20+10+10+10 = 50; total = 90; C4 = 55.6%, "in the middle." HHI example: shares 30/30/20/20 → 10,000 × (.09+.09+.04+.04) = 2,600.
  • Concentration alone is only a weak predictor of profit in real data, because of the other forces.
Her take (9/16, 10/28)

"On the exam I will not ask you to do calculations, but you do need to have intuition." Intuition: with one firm, 1 ÷ 1 = 100%. When each firm is tiny, the top four add up to almost nothing, so C4 is near 0. Fewer firms, higher C4. For HHI: "you don't need to memorize the formula, but you do need to know it's a way to capture industry concentration."

How to define the industry you are in

No single best way. Use several: (1) classification codes (NAICS, SIC), (2) how organizations describe themselves, (3) the "what job are customers hiring you to do?" test (jobs to be done). The job test reveals substitutes. For a pickleball club in Provo, the job is exercise, recreation and meeting people, so look beyond pickleball for rivals. BMW example from your Quiz 1: identifying the job identifies the most strategically relevant competitors.

The Five Forces (+ complements)

You are the firm in the middle; you want to weaken every force. For each, ask who you have to share profits with.

ForceStrong (bad for you) when…Examples from class
RivalryMany direct competitors · slow or no growth · high exit barriers (non-redeployable assets) · high fixed-to-variable cost ratio · little differentiation · low switching costs · excess capacityBowling alleys, steel (exit barriers); airlines, theme parks (fixed costs, fill empty seats); streaming (saturated, low switching costs)
Buyer powerFew buyers · alternatives available · easy to switch · buyers could backward integrate · buyers are price sensitive. "The party that can walk away has the power."Walmart/Costco over suppliers; student renters (low-medium)
Supplier powerFew suppliers · differentiated inputs · high costs of switching suppliers · you are a small share of their sales · suppliers could forward integrate. Mirror image of buyer power.Harry Potter actors (power grew each film); Boeing and Airbus over airlines
Threat of entryWeak when barriers to entry are high: economies of scale/scope · capital requirements · customer switching costs · access to scarce resources (shelf space) · regulations/patents · learning curve · network effects · expected retaliation by incumbentsSocial media: entry is easy, scale is hard (network effects). Virgin Cola failed against Coke and Pepsi
Threat of substitutesA product serves the same function with different characteristics (email vs letters vs fax). A rival has largely the same characteristics (Gmail vs Yahoo).Plant-based vs traditional meat (medium); Coke and Pepsi buy new brands to neutralize substitutes
Complements ("sixth force")Products that increase the value of your product when used togetherEV charging stations; a laptop bundled with an external hard drive
Exam traps
  • High barriers to entry = LOW threat of entry. Same force, two opposite phrasings. The practice question about the "least profitable" industry hinges on this: the right answer has two strong forces (high buyer power, high substitutes).
  • Number of competitors is only one factor in rivalry. FinTech with 1,000 firms is not necessarily more rivalrous than AI with 700, because growth rate and differentiation matter. "Please do remember this."
  • Fragmented industries: more competitors and more intense rivalry. Rapid growth does not increase rivalry; slow growth does.
  • Switching costs: the lower they are, the easier to poach customers. High switching costs weaken buyers, raise entry barriers, and soften rivalry (loyalty cards, Delta/United tiers). They matter for every force.
  • Hershey buying sugar: Hershey is the buyer, so that's buyer power, not supplier power. Decide whose perspective you are taking.
Her take: ways to weaken a force (useful for short answers)
  • Rivalry (Netflix CEO): differentiate (exclusive content), consolidate/merge, impose switching costs.
  • Buyer power (student renters): shorten contracts and shrink deposits (lower switching costs), fewer students/more housing, or backward integrate.
  • Supplier power (studio vs actors): recast with a story that justifies it (Batman), or sign young, cheap actors to long-term contracts.
  • Entry (challenger to TikTok): seed the network with influencers, subsidize early users, don't look like a direct threat at first.
  • Substitutes (Tyson): praise your own product, make the substitute yourself, buy it, attack it, or fight over labeling so consumers stop seeing it as a substitute.

Coke / Pepsi in the Five Forces (9/23 and the Coke case)

Her verdict: all five forces are weak for Coke, so the industry is very profitable. Taste is not the explanation (a generic cola wins blind taste tests every time).

  • Suppliers: little power; inputs like sugar are basic commodities and Coke can switch.
  • Buyers (bottlers): little power; bottlers build plants next to Coke with heavy investment and depend on the brand.
  • Entrants: weak threat; shelf space is scarce, fountain contracts and bottler relationships are locked up, brand loyalty (Virgin Cola failed).
  • Rivalry: weak; Coke and Pepsi coexist, rarely price-war, and have divided up restaurants.
  • Substitutes: many exist, but Coke buys new brands to neutralize them.
  • Case numbers: top three firms hold over 80% of U.S. volume. Concentrate makers earn 20–30% margins vs 8–15% for bottlers (concentrate is low capital intensity, rests on intangible assets, and the maker has leverage over bottlers).

Broader forces: PESTEL

Political · Economic · Socio-cultural · Technological · Environmental · Legal. These constrain the firm and can change the five forces. Coke example: health-conscious consumers (socio-cultural), water use and plastic (environmental), tariffs (political/economic), GDPR-style data laws (legal), AI (technological). Patent protection laws are political/legal; population demographics are socio-cultural; currency exchange rates are economic; manufacturing technology is technological.

Her take (9/16)

External forces constrain but don't remove agency. Best Buy embraced Amazon as a permanent constraint and asked how to coexist. And do not judge an industry on average profitability alone: with a unique value you can neutralize forces (the "next Trader Joe's" in grocery).

4. Internal analysis

Study guide: value chain; resource-based view (VRIO); resources vs capabilities; tangible vs intangible; inimitability; path dependence; causal ambiguity; time compression diseconomies; social complexity; network effects; strategic fit.

Value chain

The set of steps a firm takes to turn inputs into outputs (design → production → distribution → marketing/sales). It shows what a firm is good at. It cannot tell you why Zara beats H&M; for that you need resources and capabilities. Zara: vertically integrated, garments in store in about three weeks (industry average about six months), almost no advertising, few markdowns.

Resource-based view (RBV)

  • Resources = things an organization possesses. Tangible: physical assets, financial resources, technology. Intangible: brand/reputation, knowledge, human capital, culture, relationships.
  • Capabilities = things an organization can do: McDonald's delivers fast consistent service at scale; Toyota builds high-quality vehicles; Apple designs intuitive products. Resources are the ingredients, capabilities the recipe.
  • Core assumption: resource heterogeneity. Firms hold different bundles of resources and capabilities, and that explains performance differences within an industry.
  • RBV/VRIO identifies what a firm is better at than competitors (the value chain only shows what it is good at).
  • Her guiding principle: ask "how easily can someone copy us?" Combinations of resources are much harder to copy than any single one.

Her take (9/23, 10/28): "you need to know this"

"To have a competitive advantage you have to have valuable and rare resources, but to have a sustained competitive advantage you have to check all four boxes." I and O make it last. On the practice AI question she said you can argue yes or no for full credit, as long as you show you know what the boxes mean: all four checked = sustainable; only V and R = temporary. (She sometimes says "BRIO"; it's the same test.)

  • "O" failures: training staff you can't retain; a platform with huge user value and no way to monetize; Seaterz (best equipment but no system to retain profits). Taylor Swift is her favorite "O" example: she captured the value of her music by re-recording her catalog (Taylor's Version).

Exam traps
  • Market share is not a barrier to imitation (practice question #2). Neither is "rarity" (that's a VRIO test).
  • One barrier can be copied; the "ideal case" is as many barriers as possible (Ferrari uses most of them).
  • Causal ambiguity = can't tell what causes success. Social complexity = can tell but can't replicate the relationships. Time compression = can't hurry it.
  • Network effects appear twice: as a barrier to imitation here and as a barrier to entry in Five Forces.

Strategic fit and the company pyramid

Alignment is "so, so crucial." The company pyramid asks whether the layers reinforce each other or pull apart: values and priorities → resources and capabilities → activities (what we actually do). Chipotle is well aligned (culture, training, selective franchising); Boeing's cost-pressure shortcuts contradict its engineering values. Alicia (the "Full Plate" case) is misaligned: she values family, creativity and connection with God, but works overtime, uses a job that is all metrics, and does everything alone despite capable people around her. Harley can't copy Honda because the choices must fit together.

5. Generic strategies

Study guide: economic value; competitive positioning (cost leadership and differentiation); sources of cost advantage; (dis)economies of scale; economies of scope; scale, learning and experience curves; sources of differentiation; jobs to be done.

Economic value (draw this picture)

Economic value = willingness to pay (WTP) − cost. Price sits between them. Two routes to more value:

  • Cost advantage: a similar product at lower cost (lowers the cost line).
  • Differentiation advantage: a unique product earns a price premium (raises WTP).
  • Innovation can do both at once. Usually the goal of business model innovation.

Positioning grid: scope (broad vs narrow) × source of advantage (cost vs differentiation) gives broad low-cost, broad differentiation, focused low-cost and focused differentiation. Trying to do both without choosing = stuck in the middle.

Sources of cost advantage (the five)

SourceIdeaExample
Economies of scale and scopeScale = be big: spread fixed costs over more units, allow specialization. Scope = be broad: share costs across products or businesses.Smartphone volume (scale); Gap and Banana Republic sharing costs (scope)
Learning and experienceCost per unit falls as cumulative output grows.Pizza shop
Proprietary knowledgeProduction know-how others lackToyota practices
Lower input costsBargaining power, cooperation, low-cost locationsNike in Asia (<$0.50/hr) vs Adidas in Europe ($15–20)
Doing things differentlyA different business model, value chain or activity setCostco's warehouse-as-store model

Curves and limits

  • Scale curve: cost per unit vs volume in a given year. Experience curve: cost per unit vs cumulative volume. (Your Quiz 3.)
  • Learning curve = labor costs only (workers get more efficient). Experience curve = all costs (the organization gets more efficient: lower waste, better supplier prices). Law of experience: cost per unit falls by a constant percentage (typically 10–30%) each time cumulative output doubles.
  • Diseconomies of scale: past the minimum efficient scale, growth adds complexity and per-unit cost rises. Netflix expanding abroad needs translation and local content, so costs go back up. Practice question: true.
Her take (9/30, 10/28)
  • "Economies of scale means be big... economies of scope says be broad." Don't mix them.
  • Low cost ≠ low price. Firms price low for strategic reasons (win share, drive rivals out). You must look at cost structure, so you can't call the cheapest grocer a cost leader just from prices. (She said even many MBAs miss this.)
  • Strategy is interdependent choices; use systems thinking. Costco: even if members would pay more for broader selection, more SKUs reduce buying power, need more space, raise inventory and handling costs, cause errors, and kill the "treasure hunt." "If we put something in, we have to take something else out." Costco leverages all five cost sources (warehouse model, ~2,000-3,000 SKUs, owns chicken farms, high wages that cut turnover, membership data for forecasting).

Differentiation

  • Goal: raise willingness to pay. Four primary ways: superior features, reliability/quality, convenience, brand/image. (Economies of scale is NOT one of them.) Examples: Rolex (brand), FedEx (reliability), Trader Joe's, Lululemon, Muji.
  • Segment the market first: by product attributes, customer attributes, or job to be done.
  • Jobs to be done has three dimensions: functional ("help me do"), emotional ("help me feel"), social ("help me seem"). She said emotional and social jobs are often more important than the functional one; people decide emotionally and justify functionally. The job also defines who your competitors are (BMW example).
  • NPS tells you if it is working: % promoters (9–10) minus % detractors (1–6). Unintended consequences of differentiation: rising expectations, more comparison, choice overload, constant upgrading. Luxury brands (Hermès, Rolex, Louis Vuitton) create value beyond function through image and scarcity.

Her take: "What's the best strategy?"

The one you execute well, that is coherent, and that makes you unique.

Harley vs Honda (she said "if you see something like this on the test, are you comfortable with the differences?") Harley: differentiation based on identity, culture and community for (older, mostly American) riders; customization, parts, apparel and financing extend the relationship; emotional and social jobs (riders even tattoo the brand). Honda: cost leadership; functional job; global reach and scale (about 20.6 million units vs Harley's about 124,000). Harley cannot copy Honda: it lacks scale and learning curve, and a cheap Harley would dilute the identity that justifies its premium. Borrowing one element of another firm's strategy has to be checked against the rest of your choices.

6. Innovation strategy

Study guide: incremental vs radical; sustaining vs disruptive; first-mover vs second-mover; exploration vs exploitation; creative destruction; industry life cycle; platforms vs pipelines; 3 business model pillars; blue ocean vs red ocean; bottom of the pyramid; frugal innovation.

What counts as innovation

Four types: product, process, managerial, business model. Each can be incremental (small change: mobile check-in at a hotel, a new phone model) or radical (large change: Airbnb replacing hotels). "Innovative strategy = reinventing the business model." Why it matters: 83% of executives rank innovation a top-three concern; Nokia looked invincible in 2007. Gary Hamel: "you've got to out-innovate the innovators."

Three business model pillars (core exam item)

PillarContainsChanging it changes…
Value propositionWho (clients), what (products and services), priceRevenue
Value architectureValue chain, resources, capabilities (how value is delivered)Costs and capital employed
Profit equationRevenue, costs, capital employed (asset-heavy vs asset-light)ROCE (the financial result)

The value proposition can serve several parties (meal vouchers: employers, employees, restaurants). Kilimo Salama redesigned every step of the value architecture: sell crop insurance where farmers buy seed, take payment by phone, and trigger payouts from weather stations, avoiding costly individual claims.

Don't memorize the 14 directions

She said she'll never ask you to list them. Know the idea and a couple of examples: reduce clients' costs (freemium, pay-per-use, fractional ownership, third-party payer), reduce hassles (Venmo, Neighbor), look for non-clients, add functionality or emotion, search other segments or industries (recombine: Topgolf, hostel + co-working), change the revenue stream (subscription, income-share), introduce a third party (a platform). Seven directions are for value proposition, seven for value architecture.

Blue ocean vs red ocean

  • Red ocean: fight rivals in an existing, crowded market with marginally different products ("blood").
  • Blue ocean: create new, uncontested market space with a unique value proposition, making competition irrelevant. Typically both lower cost and higher value.
  • Tools: the strategy canvas (plot how much of each attribute you offer; your "value curve") and the ERRC grid (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create). Formule 1 hotels: just great sleep, nothing else. Cirque du Soleil and Nintendo won by addressing non-clients.

Bottom of the pyramid (BOP) and frugal innovation

  • BOP: serving the largest, poorest group of consumers, who are often non-clients because they can't afford current offers.
  • Frugal innovation: radically lower-cost, simple, robust solutions (Embrace baby incubator: a hot-water pouch that keeps premature babies warm).
  • The paradox (Quiz 4): pure low-cost products fail because buyers don't want a reminder that they are poor. Fix: invest in design and add aspirational value, so the product feels dignified, not cheap.

Creative destruction and the industry life cycle

  • Creative destruction: new ways of doing things replace old ones (ice men → refrigerators, typewriters, Redbox). Business models, products and whole industries get replaced.
  • Life cycle stages: introduction → growth → shakeout → maturity → decline. Not every industry follows all five (disruption can end the path early).

DeclineShrinking demand; consolidation and exit.

Her line: "your strategy is going to depend on where you are as a company and as an industry."

Sustaining vs disruptive innovation

  • Sustaining: improvement along the same trajectory for existing customers.
  • Disruptive (Clayton Christensen): starts at the bottom, inferior but affordable; existing customers don't want it and incumbents don't see a threat; over time it improves and overtakes. "One of the most dangerous types of players, players you don't see as rivals." Hard to detect; by the time you notice, it's too late.
  • Why incumbents fail to respond (her two categories): (1) they underestimate emerging innovations (the early value of something new is hard to see); (2) disruption is often competence-destroying and cannibalizes the existing business (Kodak invented the digital camera but feared it would kill film; Blockbuster profited from late fees, so it couldn't go all in on streaming).
  • How to reduce vulnerability: challenge mental models (diversity of perspectives, outside eyes, not just past data; Fosbury flop), experiment and learn from failure (question assumptions → form hypotheses → experiment → learn and adapt; Netflix runs thousands of experiments daily), and combine exploitation and exploration. Also understand non-customers.
Exam trap

Disruption does not begin by attacking incumbents' best, highest-margin customers. That is sustaining innovation.

First mover (pioneer) vs second mover (follower)

First-mover advantagesFirst-mover disadvantages (= second-mover advantages)
Above-average returns until rivals respond · start down the learning/experience curve earlier · customer loyalty · set the industry standardDemand uncertainty (cost of educating customers) · free-rider effects / leapfrogging · risk of backing a losing standard
Her take (10/14)

"We can't say one is better than the other." In her data the follower often wins (Boeing 707 over the Comet, IBM over Xerox, Apple/Samsung over Nokia/BlackBerry), sometimes the leader (Polaroid over Kodak, P&G diapers), sometimes it's unclear. Mnemonic: the advantages of being a pioneer are the disadvantages of being a second mover, and vice versa.

Exploration vs exploitation

Exploitation = scale up what you're good at (efficiency, refine the core). Exploration = put resources toward a "question mark," experimenting with new opportunities. Both extremes fail: Lego explored so much it nearly went bankrupt; firms that only exploit get disrupted. "They can't just choose one. They have to do both." Ways to balance: intrapreneurship (80% core / 20% own projects), a separate "sandbox" unit with different incentives, corporate venture capital. IKEA balanced furniture (exploit) with a home-technology lab (explore).

Her take: AI and advantage (10/14, Quiz 5)

AI gives operational efficiency, "sustainable competitive advantage, not really." Everyone using the same tool gets the same polished, homogenized output (she asked about countries and kept getting Japan). Output everybody can access is not rare or inimitable. This is the logic behind the coffee-chain AI practice question.

All three layers (industry, business unit, corporate) shape profits.

Firm scope: three ways to widen it

  • Vertical integration: take on other stages of the industry value chain. Backward = toward inputs/suppliers (Walmart's private label); forward = toward distribution/customers (Apple selling directly; Coke into bottling).
  • Horizontal (product) diversification: new products or services (Disney: movies → streaming → parks → cruises).
  • Geographic diversification: new places (Harley into China: low-cost rivals, noise rules, scrapping rules; its value proposition doesn't travel).
Better-Off Test (when to diversify)

Are the businesses worth more together than separately? NPV(B1, B2) > NPV(B1, 0) + NPV(0, B2). If yes, diversify; if not, don't. "When does one plus one equal three?" Synergies are hard to predict, and the costs of integration (especially merging cultures) are almost always underestimated.

  • Related diversification succeeds more often than unrelated (General Mills, "meeting the needs of the homemaker," drifted into clothing and jewelry and later spun off units). Performance is highest at related-constrained diversification. Today firms like Kellogg, J&J and Kraft Heinz are splitting up to focus.
  • Disney and gaming: avoided it in 2016 (culture, diverted resources), recommitted later because gaming became core to entertainment. Lesson: reassess scope over time. Synergy: kids meet characters in games and want merchandise and parks. Cost: brand harm from a poor game, culture clash.
  • BCG growth-share matrix: stars (high share, high growth: invest), cash cows (high share, low growth: milk), question marks (low share, high growth: analyze, grow or divest), dogs (low/low: divest). Lesson: don't treat all units the same. Limits: assumes units are independent (a "dog" may support a "star"), share isn't profit, emerging businesses look bad.

Firm boundaries: make, buy or ally

Best-Alternative Test

Is ownership actually required, or can another arrangement (alliance, JV, contract) deliver equal or greater value?

The rule: use the market when possible → if market transactions are difficult or risky, use alliances and contracting → if those are insufficient, vertically integrate.

Her take (10/21)

"What is the default option? Markets" (markets are efficient; you can't be good at everything; you stay flexible). The market fails when you can't specify quality in a contract (brand risk; incentive misalignment) or when only a few suppliers hold power over you. Disney and gaming: not the market (can't contract quality), not fully make (not a core competency), so closer partnerships, not hands-off licensing. The rule also applies to platforms (WeChat built, bought and partnered).

  • Make = build internally (own studio). Buy = source from the market (hire a studio, or acquire). Ally = partner, JV.

Global strategy

  • Why go global: growth, efficiency/scale, managing risk, learning (McDonald's brings overseas menu ideas home). Yet on average companies that stay local outperform those that go global, because of:
  • Liability of foreignness: extra costs of operating in an unfamiliar environment, even with a superior product (a French shop expects you to say hello first; Home Depot, Walmart and Target lost money abroad).
  • CAGE distances (the smaller, the better): Cultural (language, norms, ethnicity, religion), Administrative (government policy, institutions), Geographic (remoteness, size, time zones, infrastructure, climate), Economic (income differences).

Moral limits of markets (Sandel, "What Money Can't Buy")

Two arguments against turning everything into a market transaction, as she presented them on 10/28:

  1. Fairness / inequality: if everything is for sale, people with more money get more.
  2. Corruption of values (changing motivation): a market norm can crowd out an intrinsic one. Paying kids to read books makes it a job; they pick the shortest books to maximize pay and lose the love of reading. Hiring a "cuddling buddy" turns connection into a transaction.

"Everybody makes their own choice. We're going to choose where the line is for us."

Maui Nui (case)

Concepts it illustrates (you don't need case facts): harvest volume is capped by deer, landowner access, inspection and cold-chain, so growth is constrained. A cross-subsidy (premium mainland prices fund affordable local prices and community programs). Its social license / legitimacy and promises (kuleana, responsibility) matter as much as efficiency, so allocating each extra pound is a non-market, values-consistency decision. The question "where should each additional pound go?" has no single right answer; a valid argument that names the trade-off wins. Fresh and frozen cuts carry local relationships; shelf-stable snacks travel and bring premium revenue.

Red Queen effect: the thin spot

The study guide lists it, but I found it in none of the slides, case files or transcripts. This is the standard meaning, not her wording: from Through the Looking-Glass, "it takes all the running you can do to keep in the same place." Competitors adapt to each other, so your improvement gets matched and no one gains lasting relative advantage. In a non-market setting: when one firm increases lobbying or CSR, rivals match it, producing an escalating arms race with little net advantage. Ask Andrew at tomorrow's review how she defines it.

Practice scenario 1: Summit Pickle

Summit Pickle runs a paid indoor pickleball club in Provo: 12 courts, a coffee bar and a ladder-league system that matches members by skill. Membership is $60 a month. Three other indoor clubs are opening within five miles. The owners are considering (a) adding a pro shop and apparel line and (b) opening a second club in Boise, Idaho.

  1. What industry is Summit Pickle really in? Give one direct rival and one substitute.
  2. Assess any two of the five forces and say whether the industry looks attractive.
  3. Use VRIO to evaluate the ladder-league system as a source of advantage.
  4. Is Summit Pickle better off pursuing cost leadership or differentiation? Name one source.
  5. Choose (a) or (b) and apply the matching test or framework.
Show model answers
  1. Industry. Use the jobs-to-be-done test: customers hire it for exercise, recreation and meeting people, so it competes in social fitness and recreation, not just "pickleball." A direct rival is another indoor pickleball club; a substitute is a gym class, a rec league for tennis or basketball, or the free outdoor courts, because they do the same job with different characteristics.
  2. Forces. Rivalry is high: three clubs are opening nearby, service is similar and member switching costs are low. Threat of entry is high too: courts are cheap to build in a warehouse and members can leave anytime (low barriers). With strong forces, the average club will earn modest profits, so the industry looks unattractive unless Summit differentiates.
  3. VRIO. The league is valuable (members stay for matches) and somewhat rare, but a rival can copy a ladder system quickly, so it fails inimitability. It is not organized to lock in value unless Summit builds community and data around it. Verdict: at most a temporary advantage. (You could argue yes if the member community is hard to copy: social complexity and network effects across skill levels.)
  4. Generic strategy. Differentiation. Rivals have the same courts, so Summit can't win on cost without scale. Source: the community, the skill-matched leagues and the coffee bar (convenience and brand), which lift willingness to pay. Fit matters: a discount would conflict with a community-premium identity.
  5. (a) Apparel. Apply the Better-Off Test: is a pro shop worth more with the club than separately? Synergy: members already identify with the brand, so apparel and equipment sells at low acquisition cost (related diversification). Costs: inventory, new capabilities and managers' attention. Do it only if combined value clearly exceeds separate value. (b) Boise. Geographic diversification: check CAGE distance (small, so good) and liability of foreignness (no local community yet). Use the make/buy/ally rule: franchise or partner with a local operator before owning.

Practice scenario 2: TutorMatch

TutorMatch is an app where BYU students can book paid tutoring from other students. At launch, few tutors signed up because there were few students, and few students signed up because there were few tutors. A traditional tutoring center nearby has dismissed the app as "a toy." TutorMatch is considering adding a $5 monthly subscription for students.

  1. Is TutorMatch a pipeline or a platform? Explain why that matters.
  2. Name the problem it faces at launch and propose one solution.
  3. Give one reason a rival would struggle to copy TutorMatch once it has many users.
  4. Which business-model pillar does the subscription change?
  5. Why might the tutoring center be making a mistake by dismissing the app?
Show model answers
  1. Platform. It connects two user groups (students and tutors) rather than producing the service itself. That means its value comes from the participants and it depends on network effects, unlike the center, which employs its own tutors in a linear chain.
  2. Chicken-and-egg problem: each side joins only if the other side is already there. Solution: give one side single-user utility or subsidize it (e.g. recruit tutors from a department with guaranteed first bookings, or give students free first sessions), and start with one niche community (a single course or major) before expanding.
  3. Network effects raise the barrier to imitation and entry: more tutors attract more students and vice versa, so a copycat with few users offers less value. Switching costs (stored reviews, booking history) add to it.
  4. The profit equation / value proposition: a subscription changes the revenue stream (and price in the value proposition), which in turn affects the profit equation.
  5. This looks like a disruptive innovation: cheaper, more flexible and initially inferior to the center, serving students who find centers too costly or inconvenient. Incumbents often underestimate such entrants, and the app could improve and overtake. Responding may also cannibalize the center's existing model.
