# LECTURE 1: 2026-09-23 (Five Forces wrap-up, Value Chain, Resource-Based View, VRIO/BRIO)

## 1. Concepts taught

**A. Five Forces: what it is for (review, 0:02:07-0:02:40).** External analysis says "we're not the only company here." We must share the value created with buyers, suppliers, substitutes, and so on. For each player ask: how much power do they have, and how much value do we have to give up? More value given up = less profitable; weaker forces = we keep more. "That's the essence of the five forces."

**B. Five Forces explains BETWEEN-industry, not WITHIN-industry, profitability (0:13:25-0:14:32). This is the key conceptual point of the first half.**
- Her intuition: a new substitute or new entrant threatens *everybody* in the industry, Coke and Pepsi alike. If you ran five forces on Pepsi you would get "the exact same thing" as for Coke. So five forces gives you "the average profitability in that industry."
- "You cannot know why Coke might be less or more profitable than Pepsi. Because this is within industry variation." (0:14:04)
- It can answer why one industry beats another. Example: Coke's industry vs airlines (which lose money). Airlines have only two aircraft suppliers, Boeing and Airbus, "so they have a lot of power" (0:14:20-0:14:32).
- To explain Coke vs Pepsi you have to look inside the companies: their resources and the strategies they pursue (0:14:39-0:14:49).
- She says even "a lot of MBA students get confused on" this (0:13:25). Treat as a likely trap question.

**C. Why you can't explain Coke's profit by taste (0:05:16-0:11:00).** Taste tests: given Coke, Diet Coke, Pepsi, Diet Pepsi, and a generic cola, the generic gets the most votes "every single time." Better explanation is industry structure: "if something is highly profitable, what prevents others from doing the same thing?" and "why do they get to keep most of the profitability and not give it away to suppliers and buyers?" Also: the money is in the **concentrate** (the core business), not bottling (0:06:32).

**D. Value chain (0:20:36-0:27:00).** "The set of steps that a company takes to convert [raw] inputs into final outputs." It tells you what a company is good at. **Limit:** a value chain does not tell you why Zara is better than H&M (0:26:49). For that you need resources and capabilities.

**E. Resource-Based View (RBV) (0:26:59-0:27:35).** "One of the most important theories actually in strategy": resources and capabilities are what enable a firm to do something unique.
- **Analogy: resources = the ingredients; capabilities = the recipe**, i.e., how you combine the ingredients (0:27:17-0:27:30).
- Resources are tangible and intangible ("we'll come back to this"; **[GAP]** not elaborated in these transcripts).
- Intangibles can matter as much as tangibles; for countries, intangibles/brand matter (0:55:05).

**F. Prioritizing limited resources: the guiding principle (0:32:03-0:32:58).** Fast-food firm with limited funding: ask what maximizes *sustainable* competitive advantage. "How easily can someone else copy us?" Her key insight: **"combinations are a lot harder to copy"** than any single thing (0:32:31-0:32:45). Then: "which resources check the VRIO boxes?"

**G. VRIO / "BRIO" (0:32:58-0:34:35, 0:50:13-0:50:32).**
- **V**aluable: helps you create goods people will pay money for.
- **R**are and **I**nimitable: lets you do things other companies cannot.
- **O**rganized to exploit: is the company actually set up to capture the resource's value? Examples of failing O: a firm spends heavily training people but can't retain them (low retention, human capital not utilized); platforms with lots of user value but no way to monetize (0:33:21-0:34:09).
- **Mnemonic / rule: V and R give you a competitive advantage; I and O make it sustainable ("I and O ensure that your competitive advantage lasts")** (0:34:13-0:34:30). Sustained competitive advantage needs all four boxes checked.
- She uses "BRIO" for the same test. **[UNCLEAR]** what "B" stands for; at 0:50:28 she says "valuable, rare, and enabled, and organized to exploit" (garbled; the first letter is probably Valuable/Beneficial, and "enabled" is probably "inimitable"). The substance matches VRIO.

**H. Barriers to imitation = the "I" (0:34:34-0:37:05).** "The letter I is so important." Listed barriers:

**H. Differentiation (0:42:26-0:46:05).** The goal is to "raise this bar" (willingness to pay): convince customers to pay more. **Four primary ways to differentiate: superior features** (does a better job or more jobs), **reliability, convenience, brand and image** ("goes beyond the functional... how do I look when I wear this").

**I. Market segmentation (0:46:08-0:48:03).** Customers want different things (cars: mileage, price, 4WD, color/safety); companies segment the market into groups with similar needs. **J. Pros and cons of generic strategies (0:48:23-0:52:20).** Cost leadership vs differentiation, and broad vs focused:
- **Narrow/focus:** upside = you can focus and make products customers love; downside = "all your eggs in one basket" (downturn hits your segment), and the rest of the market is wide open to others.
- **Broad:** trying to please everybody, run out of resources, end up with "a lot of OK products," spread too thin.
- **Cost leadership risk:** race to the bottom; temptation to keep dropping prices until a price war leaves nobody making money.
- **Differentiation risk:** willingness to pay is high but customers are picky and won't buy if the product is less than perfect.
- **"What's the best strategy?" -> the one you execute well, that is coherent, and that makes you unique from the rest of the class** (0:52:08-0:52:18).

**K. Jobs to be done (JTBD) (0:52:23-0:54:20).** Another way to segment: understand *what customers are buying the product for*. Jobs can be **functional, emotional ("how do I want to feel?"), or social ("how do I want to appear?")**. Emotional and social are "a lot of times more important than the functional": people decide emotionally/socially, then justify with functional benefits. Applied to job hunting: employers "buy" you for emotional/social reasons (are you fun to work with? will you represent the company well? will you take things off my plate or complicate my job?). Identifying the emotional/social driver lets you offer a better solution. A separate assigned video covers JTBD in detail.

**L. NPS and the differentiation paradox (0:54:33-0:58:12).** "How likely are you to recommend...?" NPS counts promoters vs detractors and ignores the middle. **D. Innovative strategy = reinventing the business model.** Business model = "a rationale for how a company creates and also delivers value" (0:12:39-0:12:51).
**Three pillars (0:12:53-0:13:49), a core exam item:**
1. **Value proposition**: what value is created and for whom (clients, products, price).
2. **Value architecture**: how value is delivered (value chain, resources and capabilities).
3. **Profit equation**: the financial implications of the first two. Changing the value proposition changes **revenue**; changing the value architecture changes **costs and capital employed**. Capital employed matters (asset-heavy vs asset-light companies differ in profitability) (0:28:31-0:28:53).

**E. Value proposition: "who" and "what" (0:13:53-0:18:05).**
- *Who:* can be multiple parties. Meal vouchers in Europe: value to **employers** (employee time costs more than lunch; no need to build a cafeteria), **employees** (perk, try new places), **restaurants** (more predictable demand; guaranteed income).
- *What:* Last lecture's two sources of advantage (low cost, differentiation). **Innovative strategy tries to do both** (e.g., a differentiated car has leather seats; a low-cost car is the cheap Indian car). BlaBlaCar: very low cost for company and customers yet also differentiated (a "couple of weeks" later lecture will cover more; **[GAP]**).

**F. Strategy canvas and ERRC grid (0:18:20-0:25:23).** The strategy canvas shows visually, without knowing the business, the unique value offered: list customer-valued attributes on one axis and plot how much each model offers (the "value curve"). Formula One hotels vs 1- and 2-star hotels: reads as high quality sleep and little else, targeting traveling professionals, truck drivers, "somebody who just wants a place to sleep." **ERRC grid: create new attributes, raise some, reduce some, eliminate some.** Why cut anything? "Otherwise it would be extremely costly to offer better value on every single attribute" (0:21:03-0:21:11). Practice: HelloFresh vs home cooking: time saving (better), cost (you pay a premium), healthiness, decision fatigue (reduced), with trade-offs ("if we're saving time, we're doing something more of something else").

**G. Red vs blue ocean (0:25:25-0:26:03).** Red = "blood," lots of competition, making marginally different products. Blue = carve out new space with less competition and a unique value proposition. The strategy canvas is part of blue ocean strategy.

**H. Value architecture (0:26:07-0:28:28).** Change the value chain, resources, or capabilities. Example: Kilimo Salama (African crop insurance): traditional insurers struggled (scattered farmers, no credit cards, costly claims in remote areas). They innovated **every step**: sell where farmers already buy seed/fertilizer (distribution), take payments by phone (payment), and use **weather stations** that detect drought/flood and trigger automatic payouts (claims), which is far cheaper than processing individual claims.
