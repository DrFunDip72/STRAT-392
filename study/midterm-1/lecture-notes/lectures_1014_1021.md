#### 1.7c Exploration vs. exploitation (1:03:06-1:08:12)
- **Definitions in her words:** *exploitation* means "scaling up what we're good at." *Exploration* means diverting resources "towards something that's still a question mark." Every company must decide where to put "limited resources."
- She linked it to the **innovator's dilemma** from the earlier video: "a broader concept along the same lines."
- **Both extremes fail:**
  - Explore too much: Lego in the 90s "started exploring so much that they moved away from their bread-and-butter business, and they almost went bankrupt."
  - Exploit too much: "they get disrupted and they become irrelevant."
  - "To be successful, companies have to do both. They can't just choose one."
- **The mix is industry and company dependent.** A student said Apple's brand favors reliability over breakthrough. She agreed that established, successful companies can lean more on exploitation but "nevertheless, companies have to do some exploration."
- **Three ways firms do both:**
  1. **Intrapreneurship**, e.g., 80% of time on what the company is good at and 20% to explore on your own and report results.
  2. A **small separate unit/"sandbox"** whose people are "incentivized completely differently," not for performance, only to explore new ideas.
  3. **Corporate venture capital:** invest in startups to get information on new trends without investing heavily. If a trend succeeds, the corporation "tend[s] to acquire that startup."

## 1. Concepts taught, as she explained them

### 1.1 Business-level vs. corporate-level strategy (0:01:38-0:03:35)
- She said this returns to "question one, where to compete," answered differently, and that "we're moving away from business level strategy" to "corporate level strategy."
- **Business-level strategy:** assume one business unit. The question is "how do we create competitive advantage?" (resources, strategy within a unit).
- **Corporate-level strategy:** many companies are really "corporations" with multiple business units. The question is "how do we create value for the corporation as a whole? How do we manage all of these business units together?"
- **Why it matters:** performance has three layers: (1) external factors (five forces, macro forces; the transcript says "external partners," which is probably a transcription error for "external factors" **[UNCLEAR]**); (2) business-unit factors (resources, strategy); (3) **corporate-level effects** ("what decisions does the corporation make as a whole that also affect your performance as a business unit"). "All three sets of choices matter."
- Two broad corporate questions:
  1. **Firm scope** = what businesses should we be in?
  2. **Firm boundaries** = how do we organize operations: **make, buy, or ally?**

### 1.2 Firm scope: three ways to increase scope (0:04:14-0:06:30)
1. **Vertical integration** = "embrace a different step in the value chain, a different stage of the value chain." Example: Coke.
2. **Horizontal diversification** = "what kinds of products do we want to actually produce?" (products or services). Example: Disney.
3. **Geographic diversification** = "where do we want to compete?" Example: Harley-Davidson in China.
- She did not spell out backward vs. forward integration in this lecture. **[GAP]** For the study guide: **[NOT SAID]** moving toward inputs/suppliers is backward integration; moving toward distribution/customers is forward integration. For Coke, see the [UNCLEAR] note in Section 4.

### 1.3 The better-off test: when should a firm diversify? (0:10:15-0:12:00)
- **The test:** "Are the two businesses worth more together than separately?"
- Why it is hard: "it's not so easy to assess synergies ahead of time" and "most companies... really underestimate the costs of integration."
- **Benefits side:** synergies. **Costs side:** diverted resources/opportunity cost, brand effects, and integration costs. One cost she said is "almost always underestimated" is "the cost of integrating different cultures"; "there are almost always cultural clashes" because "companies have very unique cultures."
- Her phrasing of the goal: "when is it that one plus one equals three?"

### 1.4 Related vs. unrelated diversification (0:15:09-0:17:44)
- Success is "much higher if increasing the scope happens within related industries."
- Many firms diversify into unrelated areas, partly because of **CEO ego** ("I run a billion dollar company"). Research shows you are "much less likely to succeed if you go into things that are less related to your core business."
- **The reverse trend:** for a while companies wanted to grow as big as possible. Now firms like Kellogg and Johnson & Johnson are selling off units to focus on the core.
- **Reasons for the reverse trend (students plus her):** one segment can have much higher margins; some segments "eat away from the other parts"; it is "impossible to be good at everything"; in hyper-competition "there will be somebody who will be better than you" in a given unit, so specialize in what you are really good at.

### 1.6 Firm boundaries: make, buy, or ally; the best-alternative test (0:21:05-0:29:00)
- **The question:** "do we make something, do we buy something, or do we ally?"
- **Her rule:** the **best alternative test**: "is the ownership actually required?" (0:24:18-0:24:20)
- **Default = the market (buy).** (0:24:24-0:25:14; repeated 0:28:36-0:28:48)
  - Markets are efficient. "You cannot be good at everything," so suppliers who specialize are probably better.
  - Flexibility: if the environment changes and you have not invested, "you can just switch suppliers."
  - Usually cheaper and faster than building in-house.
- **When the market fails (market transactions are "difficult or risky"):**
  1. **Quality control/brand risk when you cannot specify quality in a contract.** "How do you put in a contract what exactly you want?" (Disney, below.) Also incentive misalignment (a student: a game studio might just "check that box and it's paid").
  2. **Few/rare suppliers** with supplier power: "if there are only like three suppliers of something, you sometimes decide, okay, we're just going to build it in-house because we don't want to be at the mercy of the supplier."
- **The middle option, ally (partnership):** Disney reasoned: not the market (quality/brand cannot be contracted), not fully make (not core competency), so partnerships. Within partnerships there is a spectrum. **Licensing** is "hands off... they just pay you money, you don't really have a lot of say." Disney is moving to **"more intimate partnerships"** with real say in how characters are developed.
- **WeChat/super app (0:28:54-0:30:12):** the make/buy/ally rule is not only for pipelines; it applies to **platforms**. WeChat (a "super app": messaging, wallet, bank, ride-hailing-style services) built some functions internally, bought some, and partnered with others, "all of the options." Garble note below.

### 1.7 Global strategy intro (0:39:31-0:47:51)
- **Why it matters to someone who "just want[s] to work for a U.S.-based company":** "every company is global to some degree because either part of the value chain is global or they have hired people from global environments."
- **Liability of foreignness:** "you pay higher costs if you are operating in an unfamiliar environment." It applies to people, sports ("we saw that last weekend"), and companies.
- **Her people-level illustration (France):** walking into a bakery or similar place without saying hello is read as treating the person "as a means to your ends... as an object" and is "deeply offensive." She has seen job applicants rejected by French bosses for not starting with hello. A European café price sign (same coffee costs far more with no greeting than with a polite "hello, one coffee please") shows the cost of ignoring local norms. "If you don't start with a hello, they will not be friendly."
- **At the company level:** "on average, companies that choose to stay local... outperform those companies that try to go global." So why go global? Reasons (hers plus students):
  1. **Growth/new opportunities** when the home market is saturated.
  2. **Efficiency/scale:** more units, "cost per unit goes down."
  3. **Managing risk:** if one country's market does poorly, others may do well (a student; she said "exactly").
  4. **Learning:** you may fail abroad but bring back changes that help the home business. McDonald's example below.
- **Where to expand:** look at four types of differences or "distances": **cultural, administrative, geographic, and economic**. She did not say the acronym "CAGE" in the transcript. Rule of thumb: "go where those distances are minimized... the smaller the differences, the higher the chance of a success." Or, if you go where the distance is large, "find a way to bridge the gap."
- **Why it matters:** "so many companies, they feel so confident, like Home Depot, Walmart, Target, and then they go abroad and they lose millions" (the transcript repeats "millions or sometimes even millions," likely meant "billions" **[UNCLEAR]**).
- **In-class exercise:** take the Blavokar or Pinduodova video cases and assess how they would do in the US, which of the four dimensions they would struggle on, and what advice to give. The class discussion was cut off, so no instructor conclusions are captured.

# Quick cross-lecture cheat sheet (only what the transcripts support)

- **Creative destruction:** new ways replace old ways (ice men -> refrigerators; typewriters; Redbox).
- **Life cycle:** introduction -> growth -> shakeout -> maturity -> decline. Early = fewer competitors, more marketing (customer education), more experimentation, growth focus. Late = hyper-competition, price pressure even for differentiators, margin extraction.
- **Disruptive innovation:** starts inferior and cheap, improves, overtakes; incumbents miss it because they underestimate it and because it cannibalizes (Kodak, Blockbuster). Sustaining = same trajectory.
- **Pioneer vs. follower:** no universal winner; pioneer wins on learning curve, above-average early returns, loyalty, standard-setting; pioneer loses on uncertainty cost, free riders/leapfrogging, losing standard. Followers' advantages mirror pioneers' disadvantages.
- **Reducing disruption vulnerability:** challenge mental models (diversity, outside eyes, not just past data); experiment (question assumptions -> hypotheses -> test -> learn -> adapt); balance exploration and exploitation (intrapreneurship, sandbox unit, corporate VC). Over-explore = Lego; over-exploit = disrupted.
- **Business vs. corporate strategy:** one unit's competitive advantage vs. value across many units.
- **Firm scope:** vertical integration (Coke), horizontal diversification (Disney), geographic diversification (Harley in China). **Better-off test:** worth more together than apart? Related diversification works better; unrelated tends to fail (General Mills); firms are refocusing (Kellogg, J&J).
- **Growth-share matrix:** stars invest; low-low divest; mixed = wait and see or milk. Limits: independence assumption (dog supports star), share is not profit, emerging businesses.
- **Firm boundaries:** make, buy, or ally. **Best-alternative test:** is ownership required? Default = market. Exceptions: cannot specify/contract quality (brand risk), few suppliers with power. Disney's compromise = intimate partnerships. The rule also applies to platforms (WeChat).
- **Global:** liability of foreignness = higher costs in unfamiliar environments. Reasons to go global: growth, efficiency/scale, risk management, learning (McDonald's). Choose markets by minimizing cultural, administrative, geographic, and economic distance (CAGE).
