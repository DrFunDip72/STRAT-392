# LECTURE 2: Sep 8, 2026 (strategic problem solving: the 4S method)

## 1. Concepts taught

### 2a. Why a method: the two traps
- Two traps: **"analysis for analysis"** (the restaurant menu for ten minutes) or **"jumping to solutions"** (hasty decisions) [0:00:47-0:01:14].
- **Experts fall in more, not less:** "as people become more expert at something they actually tend to do this sometimes even more. Because then they think... I know what has worked in the past and then they don't even do comprehensive analysis" [0:01:31-0:01:45]. "It's not a question of expertise. It's also a question of learning structure, decision making" [0:01:48-0:01:52].

### 2b. The 4S method [0:13:56-0:14:06]
**S**tate the problem, **S**tructure the problem, **S**olve the problem, **S**ell the solution.
- Failure-mode sentence: [0:14:20] "we fail more often because we solved the wrong problem, because we get the wrong solution to the right problem". It is garbled. See [UNCLEAR]. The intent is that wrong problem definition is a more common failure than a wrong answer to the right problem.
- "Well stated is half solved" [0:06:19]. "one of the most important things to do is formulate the problem correctly" [0:06:10-0:06:14].

### 2c. Step 1, State: the TOSCA worksheet (mnemonic: the opera *Tosca*)
Plot as she told it: Mario, Tosca's lover, is arrested. Tosca wants him out. She is a virtuous woman "and there are things that she will not do". Scarpia, the chief of police, wants exactly those things, and he decides Mario's fate. "How do we save Mario without yielding to Scarpia?" [0:14:41-0:14:59]. (She notes the opera ends tragically; it is only a memory device.)

| Letter | Step | Her gloss | Tosca mapping |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | **Trouble** | the symptoms; "the gap between the aspirations and the reality" | Mario is in jail |
| O | **Owner** | "whose problem is it? Because we can look at any problem from multiple perspectives" | Tosca |
| S | **Success criteria** | what success looks like | Mario out of jail |
| C | **Constraints** | what limits our problem solving | her morals |
| A | **Actors** | who else is involved and "has a say" | Scarpia (rarely just one person) |

- Purpose: after the five steps "we're in a better position to really understand what the root issue is, instead of just treating symptoms" [0:16:04-0:16:09].
- **The owner choice drives everything:** "depending on whose perspective you analyze it from, your solution space is going to look different" [0:16:42-0:16:48]. "everything else is determined by who you pick as the owner" [0:17:04]. Be precise about the owner: top management rather than "the company", because "the board doesn't always have the same interests as the top management" [0:21:41-0:21:50].
- **Write the output as a question:** "a question immediately tells your brain, problem solving" [0:24:59]. Integrate success, constraints, owner and context into one question. Her model [0:25:07-0:25:33]: "In a context where consumers are increasingly downloading pirated music and internet is becoming more accessible [trouble, constraints, actors], what actions can our company take that would result in restoring an X percent revenue growth rate with a minimum return on sales of Y percent in three years' time?"
- **Why specificity helps:** it narrows the solution space (for example, you can rule out suing consumers because you accept the market direction) [0:25:45-0:26:05]. "you are narrowing your playing field for solutions, which is the right way to do it."
- **Digital-music class exercise** (what students and she filled in):
  - Trouble: revenue dropping; no easy legal way to buy single songs [0:20:41-0:20:58].
  - Owner: the CD company, ideally top management [0:21:23-0:21:50]. An artist's view is valid but different; success there would be "we want to get paid for our music" [0:22:25-0:22:30].
  - Success: revenue goes back up with a new model [0:22:57-0:23:00].
  - Constraints: time pressure; not tech savvy ("older people running these companies, competing, like, with teenagers") [0:23:08-0:23:38].
  - Actors: companies, artists, customers who "did not want to pay extra for music they did not want" [0:23:52-0:24:04].
- She added: "People take days to try and figure out what is the actual problem... You don't have to come up with a perfect solution" [0:20:16-0:20:26].

### 2d. Step 2, Structure
- Why: problems "look so messy and daunting"; smaller questions are "more manageable"; you can **divide the work** across a team [0:26:28-0:26:53].
- **Three ways to structure.** She names the first two at [0:28:37-0:29:44] and the third at [0:55:01-0:55:25]:

| Approach | When to use | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Issue tree** (the **default**) | most problems | Core question, broken into sub-questions and sub-issues. "growing an issue tree... a default way" [0:29:33]. You explore the problem "more comprehensively". |
| **Hypothesis pyramid** (hypothesis-driven) | a yes/no question, "a lot of time pressure", or "highly confident in the solution" [0:55:05-0:55:13] | A leading hypothesis (for example, "should we expand to China?") with sub-hypotheses; if they hold, the answer is yes [0:28:44-0:29:08]. Danger: "our natural tendency to form a hypothesis right away. But when we do, most of the time we fall into one of the pitfalls" [0:29:17-0:29:28]. |
| **Design thinking** | problems "we don't understand well enough" [0:55:17] | "We need to observe, do more observation, more data collection before we can build anything and we would go to the consumer" [0:55:21-0:55:31]. |

(She does not use the word "issue-driven". Her terms are "issue tree" and "hypothesis pyramid/hypothesis-driven". Treat "issue-driven" in the study guide as the issue tree.)
- **MECE** (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive): "this is important in problem solving" [0:30:18]. Benefit (student, endorsed): "we cover all our issues, there's no doubt"; plus "we don't want different people doing the same work multiple times" [0:30:36-0:30:48]. In practice you often add an "other" bucket [0:30:56-0:31:00].
- **Frameworks help build trees.** Students ask "how do we know what the branches are going to be?" and "this is actually where frameworks come in" [0:48:35-0:48:44]. "every discipline has frameworks. And strategy does too" [0:49:45-0:49:51]. "a lot of times it's not just one... It's combining different frameworks together" [0:48:44-0:48:49]. This class will teach strategy frameworks for that purpose.

### 2e. Step 3, Solve
Project-specific, so she skips depth. List sub-issues, the analyses needed, data sources (industry reports, interviews, or assumptions "because the data is proprietary") and who owns each step [0:55:50-0:56:23]. Pitfalls:
- **Pick the right data** (GE CEO example below).
- **Make assumptions explicit.** "if you make any big assumptions, make them explicit as well" [0:59:14-0:59:22]. Assignments are "mostly effort based" [0:59:02].
- **Numbers then "so what?"**: correlation is not causation; selection effect; third factors (AI example below).
- **Survivor bias:** "who are we not looking at?" (below).

### 2f. Step 4, Sell
- **Pyramid principle:** "You start with the core message first and then you give the detail" [1:02:35]. Rationale: a manager with a thousand messages a day and a minute for yours "will get the core message".
- Cold first-contact messages: "like 20, 25" words; "if you're over 100 or 150, the probability of response goes way down" [1:02:04-1:02:35]. She calls 20-25 the low end, because you still need enough context.
- **Two organizing structures** [1:07:27-1:09:06]:
  - **Grouping:** core message plus supporting reasons (A, B, C). The familiar one; "the most common one". It can be "a little too blunt and there's no counter argument".
  - **Argument:** core message, then **situation, complication, resolution**. It "works better with skeptical audiences because... you're actually introducing more nuance". Rule: "if a message is hard to sell, argument is better" [1:09:06].
  - Recognition drill: "two reasons" is grouping. "The offer we received has advantages... However... as a result" is argument [1:08:21-1:08:53].
- Other tip from students she liked: bold the core sentence [1:06:25-1:06:43].

### 3c. Industry concentration (the first factor)
- **Definition:** concentration is about the number of players. Highly **concentrated** = very few players (examples: Google in U.S. search, "a monopolistic player"; mobile/network providers, "only a few"). Highly **fragmented** = "lots of small players and they compete against each other" (example: fast food) [0:07:35-0:08:48].
- **Rule of thumb:** fragmented industries "tend to be less profitable"; concentrated ones "tend to be more profitable" [0:08:48-0:09:03].
- **C4 (four-firm concentration ratio):** the market shares (sales) of the **four largest** firms divided by total industry sales [0:09:51-0:10:07]. Used by companies (should we enter?), governments (break up a monopoly? approve a merger?) and investors ("kind of their first clue as to whether the industry is going to be profitable or not") [0:09:22-0:09:48].
  - **Intuition:** one firm with all sales is 1 (100%). So "larger numbers means more concentrated." Near 100% is highly concentrated; near 0% is highly fragmented [0:10:13-0:10:42; 0:13:56-0:14:06].
  - **Practice (nine firms):** top four were one firm with annual sales of 20 plus three firms with 10 each, so the numerator is 50. She reports **55%** and calls it "in the middle. It's not highly concentrated. It's not highly fragmented" [0:13:27-0:14:06]. The total industry sales figure was on a slide.
- **HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index):** the measure governments use. "a little bit more sophisticated... because it uses all of the firms. And it actually gives more weight to larger ones" [0:14:34-0:14:42]. All concentration measures are "highly correlated" [0:14:27]. Governments have guidelines for low vs high concentration and may break up a firm that becomes highly concentrated; "governments don't love monopolies" [0:14:52-0:15:07].
- **Concentration alone is a weak predictor.** If it were the only factor, you would see a tight correlation. In real data "it's positive, but it's weakly correlated" because of **the five forces**, and "nowadays... the six forces" (the sixth is **complementary products**) [0:15:24-0:16:11]. Her gloss on complements: "everyone is trying to build an ecosystem... if you have charging stations, you also have electric vehicles. So things are inseparable. And so they affect each other... They also affect how profitable you're going to be" [0:16:11-0:16:33].

### 3e. The five (+1) forces, as she covered them
Setup: "We're the firm in the middle, and we want to try and weaken every force so that we should have the power in our favor, because that makes us more profitable" [0:25:16-0:25:25]. Final step: assess each force, "then you come up with the attractiveness of the industry... the more attractive it is, the more potential for making a good profit". On the Coke case, "just go through each factor... strong, weak, and then you come up with a score" [0:58:24-0:58:53].

**1. Rivalry** = "the intensity of competition" [0:15:18-0:15:24]. Drivers:

| Driver | How it works |
|---|---|
| Number of direct competitors | "help determine how much rivalry". Not the only factor. |
| Industry growth | A growing pie means new customers for everyone. A shrinking pie means fighting for existing customers, so higher rivalry. |
| Exit barriers | Hard to exit (bowling alleys: nobody to sell to, hard to convert), so firms stay even when unprofitable, so higher rivalry. |
| Fixed costs | High fixed costs (airline planes): you fill empty seats because the marginal cost is tiny, so you discount, which pushes prices down and "everybody starts making less". |
| Lack of differentiation | If customers don't see you as a rival or substitute for others (you're differentiated), rivalry goes down. |
| Switching costs | High switching costs make it hard to poach customers, so rivalry goes down. |
- **Switching-cost examples she and students gave:** the hassle of changing providers; moving apartments; **loyalty cards** ("companies... try to introduce loyalty cards because that increases the cost of switching"; Delta vs United tiers) [0:22:03-0:23:19].
- **Streaming application:** rivalry is high. The industry is "pretty actually saturated, the switching costs are actually pretty low" [0:23:39-0:23:46]. As Netflix CEO, ways to weaken rivalry: **differentiate** with unique content only on your platform; **merge/consolidate** (cut the number of competitors); **impose switching costs** [0:25:34-0:26:21].
- **Key trap (she asked you to remember it):** FinTech with 1,000 firms vs AI with 700 firms. Can you say FinTech has more rivalry? **No.** "even MBAs... will say, well, this one has more firms, so intensity of rivalry must be higher. That's not necessarily the case, because we're missing all this other information" (growth rates, differentiation, etc.). "Number of competitors is only one of the factors that helps determine rivalry. So please do remember this" [0:27:33-0:28:07].

**2. Buyer power**: "who else do we have to share our profits with?" [0:28:11-0:28:18].
- "the party that can walk away is the party with the power" [0:28:29-0:28:33]. Determinants: "Are there alternatives available? How many buyers are there? And how easy is it to walk away?" [0:28:54-0:29:01].
- **Walmart/Costco** buying from suppliers: high buyer power (huge; "if somebody loses the business of Costco... they're probably going to go bankrupt").
- **Student housing renters:** low to medium (depends how far you're willing to live) [0:29:13-0:29:21]. Ways to raise renter power: more housing or fewer students; shorten the contract in summer and negotiate a smaller deposit (both lower switching costs); **backward integrate** (provide your own housing) [0:30:32-0:32:03].

**3. Supplier power**: "a mirror image of the [buyer] power" [0:37:30]. "Whoever can walk away easily, that's the party with the power" [0:38:41-0:38:44].
- **Integration definitions (value chain: suppliers, companies, buyers):**
  - **Backward integration** = moving back toward suppliers; you become your own supplier. The buyer-side threat. Example: **Walmart** says no to a $5 price and launches a **private label**.
  - **Forward integration** = you become your own buyer/channel. The supplier-side threat. Example: **Apple** cuts out intermediaries and sells its own products [0:37:44-0:38:34].
- **Harry Potter (supplier = actor):** the slide showed actor pay per film. Conclusion: "supplier power progressively increases with each movie." By movie five he was "basically irreplaceable" because the audience was attached, "so he could name his own price" [0:40:21-0:40:45]. Studio options: recast with a story that justifies it (**Batman** has had many actors, with no backlash) [0:42:19-0:42:37]; sign **young, inexpensive** actors to **long-term contracts** that fix pay in advance [0:43:00-0:43:21]. "we want to manipulate the power of suppliers."

**4. Threat of entry**: "probably the most important one... You can prevent players from entering, then you don't have to worry about any type of battles" [0:43:34-0:43:48; partly garbled]. **Barriers to entry** she listed:

| Barrier | Her explanation |
|---|---|
| Lowest cost | An entrant "can't compete with you long-term". |
| Capital requirements | An airline: "can we borrow $10 billion? We'll get a no". An app: "I need $500. Almost anybody would qualify". |
| Customer switching costs | (see below) |
| Access to scarce resources | Coke case: **shelf space**. "if you're an incumbent and you have access to shelf space, you are in a winning position." |
| Regulations | Heavy patenting makes entry hard. |
| Learning curve | "practice makes perfect": 20 years of know-how is hard to match. |
| **Network effects** | "the value of your product goes up, the more people use it." "really important, because we live in this world of platforms". |
- **Dating-app story:** her friend (a PhD from MIT) built an app with a sophisticated matching algorithm. Result: about 200 users, 95% of them his friends, because "there are no network effects" (same for Uber and any platform) [0:46:15-0:47:09].
- **Social media application:** entry is easy ("asset light... vibe coding in one day") but scaling is hard because of network effects [0:48:00-0:48:22].
- **TikTok challenger (new short-video app):** ways to overcome barriers: bring in an **influential person** to seed a network; **take losses and pay users to join** (sports betting and prediction markets spend "billions of dollars in the first year" on sign-up bonuses; "they can't actually take off... if they don't do things like that" [0:51:30-0:52:13]); **don't look like a direct threat at first** (pretend you aren't competing with TikTok directly; more later in innovation) [0:52:21-0:52:46].

**5. Threat of substitutes.** Rivals "have the same characteristics as you". Substitutes "are a little more sneaky because they don't always look like you, they don't sound like you, but they do tend to [take] your customers" [0:53:00-0:53:18; the verb is garbled, see below].
- **Meat industry:** she rates the threat of substitutes **medium**, because it depends on who you ask: meat lovers say high-quality plant meat isn't a substitute; plant-based converts say it is [0:54:05-0:54:29].
- **Tyson's options to keep the threat low:** praise your own product (blind taste tests); make your own plant-based product (meet them at their own game); **buy the substitute** (Coke and Pepsi do this); run campaigns attacking the substitute ("do you guys even know what you're eating?... list of ingredients"); fight over **labels** (sausage, turkey) so consumers stop seeing them as substitutes and stop finding them in the same aisle [0:55:12-0:58:20].
- **Bottom line:** "you don't want the consumer to think that they are substitutes, perfect substitutes" [0:58:13-0:58:17].

**6. Complementary products** (the "sixth force"): see 3c. She gave only the EV/charging-station gloss. See [GAP].

**Switching costs run through all of it:** "Customer switching costs... this tells you that switching costs are really important... for each of the five forces" [0:44:44-0:45:08].
