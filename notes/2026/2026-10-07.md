## Summary

This lecture section covers strategic differences between Harley and Honda, emphasizing how their value propositions diverge—Harley focuses on identity and culture for older American riders, while Honda emphasizes cost leadership and global accessibility. It highlights the importance of coherent strategy, the risks of borrowing strategies from competitors, and the critical role of innovation in maintaining relevance, using examples like Nokia's decline and the need for companies to out-innovate competitors. The lecture also defines innovation broadly (product, process, managerial, business model) and introduces business model pillars: value proposition, value architecture, and profit equation.

## Key Points

- Harley offers value rooted in identity, culture, and community for older American motorcycle riders, while Honda provides financial value through cost leadership and global accessibility.
- Strategy must be coherent; borrowing elements from competitors (e.g., Honda's cost leadership) risks losing core value (e.g., Harley's identity) and may not align with existing strategic choices.
- Harley's brand loyalty is evidenced by high customer willingness to tattoo the brand, reflecting deep emotional and social commitment beyond functional value.
- Innovation is critical for relevance, as 83% of executives cite it as a top organizational concern, with companies like Nokia declining due to failure to out-innovate competitors.
- Innovation encompasses product, process, managerial, and business model changes, ranging from incremental (e.g., mobile check-in) to radical shifts (e.g., Airbnb replacing hotels).
- A business model consists of three pillars: value proposition (what value is created for whom), value architecture (how value is delivered via resources, capabilities, and value chains), and profit equation (financial implications of changes).
